# Regional CV conventions

Read when the target role's country is known. Using the wrong convention reads as carelessness, and in some markets as an outright signal the person didn't research the move.

These are conventions, not laws, and they shift. When a posting states its own requirements, the posting wins. When the person has strong reason to deviate, note the convention and let them decide.

## DACH (Germany, Austria)

- **Lebenslauf** — reverse-chronological, factual, structured as dated entries. Dense prose is not expected. "Tabular" here describes the *chronology* — a date column reading down the page — not an actual table element in the document. Build it with plain lines and consistent spacing; a real table is one of the extraction risks in `ats-and-layout.md`
- **Photo** — traditionally standard, professionally shot. Declining, and anti-discrimination law (AGG in Germany) means it cannot be required. Many larger and international employers now discourage it. Ask rather than assume; the safe default for tech and international companies is no photo
- **Date of birth, nationality, marital status** — traditionally included, increasingly omitted for the same reason. Same guidance: ask
- **Anschreiben** (cover letter) — traditionally expected and treated as substantive, not a formality. One page, addressed to a named person where possible. International tech employers hiring in the region increasingly skip it; follow the posting
- **Certificates** — degree certificates and **Arbeitszeugnisse** (employer references) are commonly attached at established German employers, where their absence is noticed. Startups and international companies frequently don't ask. Employer-dependent
- **Length** — 2 pages is normal and unremarkable
- **Signature and date** — traditional on both Lebenslauf and Anschreiben; fading in digital applications

## Switzerland

Broadly follows DACH with local differences:

- Photo is still common, more so than in Germany
- Work permit status matters and is often stated explicitly by non-citizens — this is practical information the employer needs, not oversharing
- Language competence is load-bearing. State the working language honestly, with level (e.g. CEFR). Overstating here surfaces in the first phone call
- Reference letters from previous employers are expected
- Note the canton/language region: application language for Zürich and Geneva is usually different

## UK

- **No photo, no date of birth, no marital status.** Including them is unusual and can create equal-opportunities awkwardness for the employer
- **2 pages maximum.** Firm convention
- Short personal statement at the top is common — 3-4 lines, targeted
- **References** — "available on request" or omitted; don't list contact details unprompted
- Right-to-work status is worth stating if not obvious

## US

- **Résumé**, not CV — in the US, "CV" means the academic document
- **No photo, no date of birth, no marital status, no nationality.** Employers actively avoid this information for anti-discrimination reasons; including it can make a résumé awkward to circulate
- **1 page** for early career, 2 acceptable with substantial experience
- Cover letter often optional; when requested it's short and specific
- Metrics-forward bullets are the norm and expected

## Academic applications, any country

Different genre. The industry impact bullet actively hurts. Publications, funding, teaching, and supervision carry the weight, and ordering conventions differ by field.

Length runs longer than industry but is not unbounded — universities and funders frequently impose hard limits, from page caps on fellowship applications to fixed-format biosketches. Check the specific call before assuming there's no limit.

Don't produce an industry CV with a publications section appended — say the genre is different and handle it separately.
