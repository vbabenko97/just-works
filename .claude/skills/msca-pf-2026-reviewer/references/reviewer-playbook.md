# Reviewer playbook

Use this document for detailed evaluator tests and repair logic.

## Score discipline

A 5.0 means there is no meaningful evaluator shortcoming beyond minor imperfections. If you can identify a concrete moderate or major weakness, do not award 5.0.

Do not average subsection scores mechanically. Official scores are criterion-level judgments. Use subsection analysis to justify the criterion score.

Do not treat elegant writing as a substitute for evidence, feasibility, or causality.

## Excellence 1.1: objectives, ambition, state of the art

Test whether:
- the problem is specific and important;
- the state-of-the-art gap is demonstrated rather than asserted;
- the central research question or hypothesis is explicit where appropriate;
- objectives are research outcomes, not activities;
- objectives are measurable, verifiable, realistically achievable, and logically ordered;
- ambition is relative to named comparators or limitations in the field;
- novelty claims are literature-supported and not absolute without evidence;
- negative/null results could still produce useful knowledge;
- the project is ambitious without becoming implausible for 12-24 months.

Common score losses:
- literature review without a sharp gap;
- "first-ever" language unsupported by a search;
- objectives like "develop", "explore", or "investigate" with no success/verification criterion;
- novelty equated with combining two familiar methods;
- ambition stated as societal importance rather than scientific advance;
- objectives that depend on data, recruitment, facilities, or approvals not evidenced elsewhere.

Repair pattern:
`known -> unresolved gap -> why it matters -> project question -> O1/O2/O3 with verification -> exact advance beyond comparator`

## Excellence 1.2: methodology

Test whether:
- every objective has a matching method;
- study population/data/material, variables, outcomes, controls, and analysis are defined enough to judge feasibility;
- assumptions are explicit;
- major methodological risks have alternatives;
- sample/event/data support is plausible for the planned complexity;
- validation strategy avoids leakage or circularity;
- interdisciplinarity is operational rather than decorative;
- sex/gender/diversity is integrated where scientifically relevant, or non-relevance is justified;
- open science practices improve rigor/reproducibility and are compatible with privacy, IP, and ethics;
- data management, reproducibility, code/software sharing, pre-registration, or open outputs are proportionate to the field.

Common score losses:
- methods listed but not linked to objectives;
- "state-of-the-art AI" or similarly vague method descriptions;
- missing validation, comparator, control, or uncertainty analysis;
- open science reduced to "we will publish open access";
- gender dimension confused with team gender balance;
- interdisciplinarity defined by having collaborators from different fields rather than integrating methods.

## Excellence 1.3: supervision, training, knowledge transfer

Test whether:
- supervisor expertise directly fits the scientific and training needs;
- supervision frequency, progress review, feedback, and escalation are credible;
- training closes named gaps in the researcher's profile;
- each major training item has provider, timing, practical output/evidence, and career use;
- the researcher transfers specific expertise back to the host;
- two-way transfer is distinct from routine project work;
- any secondment or non-academic placement has a clear added value and deliverable;
- the training plan supports a career transformation, not merely project completion.

Common score losses:
- generic seminars/courses;
- one-way host-to-researcher learning;
- supervisor prestige without direct fit;
- no evidence the supervisor has time or a workable supervision structure;
- training repeats skills the researcher already has;
- secondment appears bolted on for optics.

Repair unit:
`competence gap/asset -> provider/recipient -> activity -> month -> evidence/output -> project/career value`

## Excellence 1.4: researcher profile

Test whether:
- prior experience makes the project credible;
- the fellow still has clear reasons to need this fellowship and host;
- achievements are selected for relevance rather than prestige alone;
- non-linear or interdisciplinary career moves are explained constructively;
- complementarity between fellow and host is explicit.

Common score losses:
- CV copied into narrative;
- metrics without relevance;
- applicant already appears fully trained in all proposed fellowship skills;
- no clear unique contribution by the researcher to the host.

## Impact 2.1: career and employability

Test whether the proposal shows a causal transformation:
`current profile -> competence/network/leadership gaps -> fellowship activities -> evidence by project end -> plausible next career step`

Look for:
- specific target roles or career directions inside and/or outside academia;
- new scientific and transferable skills;
- leadership, supervision, project/grant management, entrepreneurship, regulation, industry, policy, or clinical translation skills where relevant;
- outputs that prove skills rather than merely attendance;
- durable network effects;
- independence gains.

Common score losses:
- "enhance employability" without mechanism;
- repeating the training list from 1.3;
- no before/after profile;
- generic desire for a faculty job with no independence milestones.

## Impact 2.2: dissemination, exploitation, communication

Keep three concepts distinct:
- Dissemination: transfer of results to specialist/research users.
- Exploitation: use/reuse of results, IP, software/data/protocol uptake, follow-on validation, policy/standard/clinical/industry pathway, future funding.
- Communication: engagement with broader/non-specialist audiences about the project, activities, and benefits.

For each important action, test:
`objective -> audience -> message/result -> channel/activity -> timing -> owner -> KPI -> follow-up/post-project continuation`

Common score losses:
- papers and conferences treated as the entire impact plan;
- communication KPIs that count impressions but not meaningful reach/engagement;
- exploitation omitted because the project is "basic research";
- no IP/open-science compatibility plan;
- no action after the project end;
- target groups described as "stakeholders" without specificity.

## Impact 2.3: magnitude and importance

Require a credible chain:
`project result -> direct users/uptake -> outcome -> longer-term impact`

Test:
- who benefits;
- why this result changes their behavior, knowledge, capability, cost, risk, or decision;
- magnitude: scale or breadth of benefit;
- importance: value/significance of that benefit;
- credible quantification where meaningful;
- realistic dependencies on future validation, regulation, adoption, or scale-up.

Common score losses:
- jumping from one fellowship to continental societal transformation;
- citing disease/market burden as if the project automatically captures that benefit;
- claiming economic impact without an exploitation route;
- claiming clinical impact from retrospective methodological work without prospective validation;
- tenuous links to SDGs or EU missions.

## Implementation 3.1: work plan, risks, effort

Trace:
`objective -> WP -> task -> deliverable -> milestone -> dependency -> risk -> mitigation/fallback -> effort`

Test:
- logical sequencing and critical path;
- realistic timing;
- specific deliverables distinct from milestones;
- verifiable milestones/decision points;
- risks that are plausible and material;
- mitigations that change the plan if the risk occurs;
- contingency for data, recruitment, approvals, technical failure, model underperformance, or supervisor/partner dependency where relevant;
- realistic effort across research, training, management, dissemination, and secondments;
- Gantt consistency with the narrative.

Common score losses:
- every risk rated low;
- risk table full of generic "delay" risks;
- mitigation is "monitor closely";
- publications used as milestones;
- too many work packages for a single fellow;
- methods and training scheduled after they are needed;
- outputs appear before prerequisite data/tasks.

## Implementation 3.2: host capacity and arrangements

Test:
- infrastructure/data/equipment/computing needed by the project are actually available or credibly obtainable;
- day-to-day integration in the group is described;
- onboarding, workspace, admin, HR, ethics/DPO, research support, technology transfer, library/data/computing, and training services are relevant and specific;
- supervisor continuity and scientific community are credible;
- B-2 capacity claims match B-1;
- host description emphasizes what enables this project, not generic rankings.

Common score losses:
- marketing copy about institutional prestige;
- facilities named but not tied to tasks;
- no concrete integration or support arrangements;
- unconfirmed data access presented as guaranteed.

## Cross-cutting red flags

Flag aggressively:
- factual contradictions across Part A/B/CV;
- placeholders and TODOs;
- "first", "unique", "unprecedented", "revolutionary" without support;
- claims that the host or partner has agreed to something when no evidence is supplied;
- excessive citations used instead of argument;
- unsupported statistics;
- mixed terminology for the same cohort, endpoint, method, WP, or deliverable;
- a training plan that is mostly conferences;
- impact language that confuses outputs, outcomes, and impacts;
- Gantt labels that do not match the text;
- risks that contradict confident feasibility claims elsewhere;
- external hyperlinks used to carry essential content;
- excessive density that makes the 10-page proposal hard to evaluate quickly.

## Page-pressure repair strategy

When the user is near the page limit:
1. remove repeated motivation and policy context;
2. compress generic host prestige and career adjectives;
3. convert repeated prose into one high-information schematic/table only if it saves space;
4. keep evaluator-critical evidence: gap, objectives, method, training logic, impact causality, risks, and host enablement;
5. cut claims that cannot be substantiated;
6. never solve page pressure by making text illegible.
