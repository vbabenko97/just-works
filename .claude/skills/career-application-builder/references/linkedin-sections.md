# LinkedIn sections

Read when producing or auditing LinkedIn content.

## Why this isn't a CV

A tailored CV is written for one vacancy and one reader who already has the job description in hand. A LinkedIn profile is read by strangers searching for people, across several adjacent role families, with no context supplied.

Two consequences:

**Keep it broad enough to stay credible across two or three role families.** Someone who could plausibly be hired as a backend engineer, a platform engineer, or an ML infrastructure engineer should not narrow the profile to whichever vacancy they applied to last week. Narrowing loses the other two without winning the first.

**It has to be self-contained.** No implied job description, no assumed context. A reader arriving from a search needs to understand what this person does within a few seconds.

Copying a tailored CV onto LinkedIn is the most common failure and it makes the profile worse than the generic version it replaced.

## Headline

The highest-leverage text on the profile — it follows the person's name into search results, comments, and messages.

- Say what they do and in what domain. The default (current job title at current employer) wastes it
- Fits roughly one line on mobile; front-load the important words
- Buzzword stacking reads as insecure. So does an unexplained "|"-delimited keyword list
- If they're open to two role families, the headline is the right place to signal both

## About

First-person, prose, 3–5 short paragraphs. The one place on the profile with room for a narrative.

Structure that works: what they do and for whom → a concrete thing they've built or solved → what they're looking for or interested in.

Avoid: third-person self-description, listing every technology they've touched, and opening with a mission statement. The first two lines are all that shows before "see more" — spend them on substance.

If a writing-style or voice reference is available, this section and outreach messages are where it belongs. CV bullets are not.

## Experience

Shorter than the CV equivalent. Two to four bullets per role, chosen for what's legible to someone outside the company.

The same status discipline applies as everywhere else — LinkedIn is public and permanent, and a colleague who was in the room can read it. An inflated claim on a CV is seen by a hiring panel; an inflated claim on LinkedIn is seen by everyone who worked on the project.

Sanitize harder than on a CV: no internal pricing, no unreleased products, no named customers without permission, no proprietary architecture detail. A CV reaches a smaller audience — but it circulates inside the hiring company and sometimes beyond, so it isn't confidential either. A profile is simply published, indefinitely, to anyone who looks.

## Skills

- Order matters — the top few are what a reader actually registers. Treat exact search-ranking mechanics as unknown; visibility and signalling are the defensible reasons to order deliberately
- List skills the person can defend in conversation. Padding is detectable in the first screening call
- Prune aggressively: a long tail of weak entries dilutes the strong ones

## Featured

Underused. Good candidates: a sanitized write-up of a project, a conference talk, a technical post, a publication, a portfolio piece.

This is where a claim gets substantiated rather than merely asserted, which is worth more than another bullet. It's also the natural home for work that's too detailed for the Experience section.

## Consistency with the CV

Titles, employers, and dates must match whatever CV the person is sending out. Recruiters cross-check, and a mismatch reads as either carelessness or embellishment.

Content need not match — the CV is narrower and more targeted by design. Facts must.
